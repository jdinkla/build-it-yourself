# A decision matrix is not a decision

A score can end an argument before anyone has understood it. Put three options in a table, add some numbers, and the highest total acquires an authority it may not deserve.

The [Decision Workbench](/tools/decision-workbench/) is useful for a different reason: it makes your assumptions visible. It holds three options and three criteria. You choose the labels, set each criterion's weight from zero to five, and score every option from zero to five. The arithmetic is simple enough to question.

## Start with a small, reversible choice

Here is an illustrative example: choosing a weekend project. Your options are a windowsill herb tray, a family photo index, and a personal link library. Your criteria might be enjoyment, usefulness, and likelihood of finishing.

Before entering scores, define the endpoints. For “likelihood of finishing,” five could mean “all materials available and comfortably fits the afternoon”; zero could mean “cannot start with what I have.” A score between them is your judgment, not a measurement made by the tool.

Keep the direction consistent: higher should always mean more desirable. If your criterion is “effort,” rename it “manageable effort,” so an easy project receives a higher score. Otherwise you can accidentally reward the thing you meant to penalise.

Set aside any option that fails an essential requirement before ranking. If an activity requires a workshop you cannot access, a high enjoyment score does not create one. Weighted scoring allows a strength in one column to offset a weakness in another; some requirements should never be traded that way.

## Read the arithmetic aloud

For each option, the workbench multiplies every score by its criterion's weight, adds those products, and divides by the sum of the weights. With positive total weight, the result stays on the zero-to-five scale. When every weight is zero, there is no weighted mean to calculate.

For example, weights of three, two, and one combined with scores of four, three, and two produce `(3×4 + 2×3 + 1×2) ÷ 6`, or about 3.33. That decimal is an accounting result. It does not mean the project is “3.33 good,” nor does a difference of 0.01 establish a meaningful advantage.

Formal multi-criteria analysis involves more work than this compact worksheet. The UK Government Analysis Function's [introductory guide](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/an-introductory-guide-to-mcda/) describes the importance of objectives, stakeholder preferences, and an appropriate value model. This tool borrows the legibility of a table; it does not perform that full process.

## Change something before believing it

Once you have a ranking, move the most debatable weight up or down by one. Then change an uncertain score. Does the leading option remain ahead? Or does a small adjustment rearrange everything?

That second outcome is useful information. It means your current conclusion depends on an unsettled assumption. You might need another fact, a trial afternoon, or a conversation with the other person affected. Official [guidance on sensitivity analysis](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-use-of-multi-criteria-decision-analysis/use-of-multi-criteria-decision-analysis-in-options-appraisal-of-economic-cases) describes testing rankings under different scoring and weighting assumptions. Here, you can do that directly by editing the inputs.

## Build a worksheet you can inspect

The recipe is three labelled columns for options, three rows for criteria, nine scores, and three weights. Keep the calculation separate from the display. Validate the allowed range. Give zero total weight an explicit empty result. Export the inputs with the totals so a future reader can reconstruct how you arrived there.

Save the exported JSON file alongside a sentence explaining the choice you actually made. Browser saving does not create a cross-device record, and this worksheet should not hold sensitive information. It is intended for ordinary, low-stakes comparisons, not decisions requiring professional advice.

## Try this: argue for the runner-up

Write one sentence explaining why you might still choose the second-ranked option. If the reason is important and absent from the table, revise your criteria. If it resists scoring, keep it in words. The table has done its job when you can explain the trade-off clearly enough to own the decision yourself.
